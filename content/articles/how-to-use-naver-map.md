Every few months someone lands here, opens Google Maps, types in a café, and gets a blue line that stops at the edge of a neighborhood. Then they stand on a corner in Seongsu holding a phone that has confidently told them they've arrived.

There was a headline in February that made it sound like this was over. It isn't, quite. Here's the actual state of things, and what to do instead.

## Quick answer

**Download Naver Map before you fly.** Add KakaoMap as a backup and Kakao T for taxis. Do it on hotel wifi, not while standing in the arrivals hall.

Google Maps is fine for *finding* a place in Korea. It is still not reliable for *getting* to one on foot or by car.

## Why Google Maps breaks here

It isn't a Google failure and it isn't about demand. It's a law.

Korea's **Act on National Spatial Data Infrastructure** restricts exporting detailed geographic data to servers outside the country. Google processes its mapping data on international servers — Singapore, Taiwan, Japan — which meant it could never access the granular Korean spatial data that domestically licensed services use.

The stated reason is security. Korea is technically still at war with North Korea, and the government has long argued that high-resolution imagery combined with precise coordinates could expose military installations.

**What that looks like on your phone:** no walking directions, no driving directions, and search results that land you at a neighborhood boundary rather than a building entrance. Which is exactly the distance that matters when you're looking for a specific door on a Seongsu side street.

## What actually changed in February 2026

On **27 February 2026**, after an eighteen-year dispute, the Korean government granted Google **conditional** approval to export high-precision map data.

The conditions are substantial: sensitive facilities blurred, the processing of sensitive data done only on domestic servers, government review before data leaves the country, an emergency shutoff, a dedicated Korea maps staffer resident in the country, and only routing and navigation data included in the export.

**And here's the part the headlines skipped.** Approval is permission, not a product. Server setup, security audits, data integration, and ongoing oversight all sit between the announcement and a working blue line. Several months on, Google Maps still lacked reliable turn-by-turn walking navigation in most Korean cities, and industry observers quoted at the time estimated **six months to a year** after approval before meaningful improvement.

So: the rule changed, the app hasn't yet. If you're reading this well after we published it, check for yourself before you decide — but **do not plan a trip on the strength of a headline.**

## Naver Map, practically

### Set it up before you go

Naver Map has an English interface. Set the language when you install it, and give it location permission — Korean map apps are close to useless without it.

**Do this on wifi at home.** Fumbling with an app store on airport wifi with a fresh eSIM is a bad first hour.

### The address problem

Korean addresses use **도로명주소** — a road-name system — which reads backwards from what most visitors expect: city, district, road, building number. Pasting an English transliteration into a search bar often fails.

**The fix that always works:** search the **Korean name** of the place, not the address. Copy-paste it. Every place page in [our Seoul directory](/seoul/places) carries the Korean name and map links for exactly this reason, and so does almost every Korean business's Instagram bio.

If you only have the English name, try it — Naver has gotten much better — but when it fails, the Korean name is the answer, not a more precise address.

### Transit is the reason to use it

This is where Naver Map is genuinely excellent, and better than anything you're used to.

- **Subway and bus routing** with live arrival times, including the walking segments at both ends.
- **Exit numbers.** This matters more in Seoul than almost anywhere. A large station can have fifteen exits spread over half a kilometre, and coming up at the wrong one costs you ten minutes and a road crossing. Naver tells you which one. Write it down.
- **Transfer optimization.** Transit directions here will often tell you which carriage to board so that you come out beside the transfer stairs. It is the single most Seoul thing about the app.
- **Bus stop IDs**, which is how you confirm you're on the correct side of a six-lane road.

### Saving and sharing

Star places as you plan. The saved list is what turns a scattered afternoon into a route — and unlike a screenshot, it updates when a business moves.

**Screenshot the last leg anyway** before you go underground. Signal in the deeper stations is fine, but the transfer corridors are where you'll actually want to look at it.

## KakaoMap, and when to switch

Install both. They're free and they disagree usefully.

**KakaoMap** is often stronger on hiking trails and some place metadata, and its search occasionally finds a small business Naver misses. If a place doesn't come up in one, try the other before concluding it has closed.

**Kakao T** is the taxi app. Taxis in Seoul are inexpensive and plentiful, and calling one through the app removes the address conversation entirely. Payment can be registered in advance, which is the whole point.

## Four things nobody mentions

**Hours on Naver are more current than Google's.** Korean businesses maintain their Naver listings because that's where Korean customers look. A Google listing for a Seoul café can be a year out of date. When they conflict, believe Naver — or better, check the business's Instagram, which is where changes actually get announced first.

**Reviews are on a different scale.** Naver and Kakao ratings cluster high, and a 4.2 is not the mild endorsement it would be elsewhere. Read the text, not the number.

**Apple Maps has the same legal problem** and is not a workaround.

**Distances are deceptive on a hill.** The map is flat; [Hannam](/articles/hannam-afternoon-local-guide) is not. A four-minute walk can include four minutes of staircase.

## The pre-flight checklist

- Naver Map, installed, English set, location on
- KakaoMap as backup
- Kakao T if you'll take taxis
- Your first day's stops saved
- The Korean name of anywhere hard to find, saved as text you can paste

Five minutes at home. It's the difference between an afternoon in Seongsu and an afternoon looking at a phone.

## FAQ

**Does Google Maps work in South Korea?**  
Partially. It works for searching and for viewing locations, but it has not offered reliable walking or driving directions, because Korean law restricts exporting detailed map data to servers abroad. Use Naver Map or KakaoMap for routing.

**Didn't Korea approve Google Maps in 2026?**  
Conditionally, on 27 February 2026 — with sensitive sites blurred, processing on domestic servers, government review, and an emergency shutoff among the conditions. Approval is not the same as a working feature; months later, turn-by-turn walking navigation still wasn't reliable, and estimates for meaningful improvement ran to six months or a year.

**Is Naver Map available in English?**  
Yes, the interface has an English setting. Searching still works best with the Korean name of a place, which is why most Korean businesses list it prominently.

**Naver Map or KakaoMap — which is better?**  
Naver Map is the stronger default for transit and general search; KakaoMap is often better for hiking trails and occasionally finds small businesses Naver misses. Install both, since they're free and they disagree usefully.

**How do I get a taxi in Seoul?**  
Kakao T. Register a payment method before you need it. Taxis are inexpensive and widely available, and using the app avoids explaining an address out loud.
